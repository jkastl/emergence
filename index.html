None of the systems below contains a picture of its result. Each is a handful of arithmetic applied over and over — yet flocks form, spots grow, spirals bloom, and chaos blossoms. The pattern lives between the rules, not inside them. Everything here runs live; poke at it.

I.

The Automaton

one dimension is enough · Wolfram, 1983

A row of cells, each on or off. Every tick, a cell looks at itself and its two neighbors — eight possible situations — and a rule is just a table saying what to do in each one. That's 256 possible universes, numbered 0 to 255.

Most are boring. A few are not. Rule 30 is so unpredictable it was used as a random number generator. Rule 110 can, in principle, compute anything your laptop can. All from a lookup table with eight entries.

II.

The Flock

no bird is in charge · Reynolds, 1986

A starling murmuration has no choreographer. Each bird follows three private urges: separate — don't crowd your neighbors; align — fly the way they're flying; cohere — drift toward their center.

No bird knows the shape of the flock. The flock is what the rules look like from far away. Move your cursor into the pen — you're a hawk.

III.

The Chemistry

how the leopard gets its spots · Turing, 1952

Alan Turing's last great idea: two invisible chemicals spreading through tissue — one feeding the reaction, one starving it — can paint spots, stripes, and labyrinths onto skin. No gene stores the leopard's pattern; the genes only tune two numbers, feed and kill, and the mathematics does the rest.

This is the Gray–Scott model, running live. Draw on it with your cursor to inject chemical, and watch the pattern negotiate the intrusion.

IV.

The Sunflower

the most irrational number in nature · 137.508°

A sunflower places each new seed at a fixed angle from the last, a little further out. That's the whole algorithm. But the choice of angle is everything: pick a rational fraction of a turn and the seeds line up in wasteful spokes. The plant needs an angle that never repeats — and the hardest number to approximate with fractions is the golden ratio.

Its angle is 137.508…°. Drag the slider even a tenth of a degree away and watch the packing fall apart into spirals and spokes. Evolution found a theorem in number theory and grew it in a field.

V.

The Pendulum

two hinges are enough for chaos · sensitive dependence

One pendulum is the physicist's favorite pet: tame, periodic, solved. Hang a second pendulum from the tip of the first and you get one of the simplest chaotic systems known. Below are two double pendulums, released from angles that differ by one ten-thousandth of a degree.

For a while they agree — then, suddenly and forever, they don't. This is why weather forecasts decay: not missing physics, but the impossibility of knowing today precisely enough to deserve next month.
